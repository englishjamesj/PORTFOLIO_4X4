Start Here

The idea, in a nutshell

Every market week, a brand-new collectible trading card appears at the booth. Each card has two sides: a FUN hero side and a LEARNING side. Kids collect the set — and keep coming back for the next one.

Your job here is to pick the feel — the Style. Everything else — the layout, the back, the info blocks — stays the same across every card.

How to navigate: tap a tab on the right to move between chapters.

How a Card Works

The anatomy of a card

This is a starting point, not a finished design. Every slot — and where it sits — is a conversation. Here's the skeleton we're proposing.

Exploded FRONT — the FUN side

Exploded BACK — the LEARNING side

The BACK is identical in structure on every one of the cards.

The back's five learning blocks are flexible — swap topics to fit each card.

The layout stays the same; the topics are yours to choose, and can differ card to card. Since you don't grow most of these varieties yourselves (other local farmers do — you specialize in specialty garlic and some peppers), Why We Grow It may not fit most cards — consider leading with the variety's story instead.

Ideas for the blocks:

  • Origins / History — the variety's heritage and how it came to be
  • Growing It — what a grower watches for (disease, spacing, timing, climate quirks)
  • Keeping It — storage and curing
  • In the Kitchen — best uses, flavor, ripeness cues
  • Did You Know — a fun or surprising fact
  • Other ideas to consider: Who Grows It Locally (spotlight a local farmer) · Flavor Notes · Best Use / "Super Power" · Seed-Saving · Companion Plants · Where to Find It

The Series Plan

How many cards, and how often?

The cadence is simple: one new card per market week.

34
weeks — the Fayetteville AR Downtown Farmers Market outdoor season. That's the theoretical max / full-series ceiling: a card every single week.

Our recommendation (your call):

Don't commit to all 34 right away. A Season-1 run of about 12–18 cards — a card every week or every other week — proves the concept and keeps "collect them all" genuinely achievable, with plenty of room to grow toward 34 later.

About this portfolio: the 4×4 = 16 cards you'll see here are a STYLE-REVIEW SAMPLE — four varieties shown in four styles so you can compare the feel. They are not the proposed Season-1 lineup.

Season 1 — Candidates

~50 varieties to choose from

A menu, not a lineup. These are strong candidates to consider for Season 1, ranked by how well each one lends itself to the cards' features — not a lineup we're picking for you. The final call is yours.

How this list is ranked

"Higher" means more card-worthy, judged on five things: (1) Visual / morph-ability — a bold, recognizable look and a face-able surface for the hero art; (2) Story hook — a real origin tale, a name, a super-power; (3) NWA-growability — genuinely happy in our 6b–7a Ozarks; (4) Set variety — spread across colors, forms, and crops so the set stays collectible; and (5) Market-weekly fit — harvests spread across the ~34-week season. Garlic ranks high on purpose: it's your specialty and it photographs beautifully.

Tags show the crop type. The top 15 get a fuller "why it's a great card"; the rest are one-liners so all 50 stay scannable. Nothing here is locked — it's yours to pick from.

  1. 1

    Chesnok Red Garlic Garlic

    The flagship — garlic is your specialty and this one's a proven card already. A premier roasting garlic (sweet and mild cooked, ~8–12 cloves, keeps 6–7 months), with translucent purple-streaked wrappers that morph into uncanny "see-through skin." Hardnecks like this thrive in our 6b winters, and the spring scape is a built-in bonus card.

  2. 2

    Cherokee Purple Tomato Tomato

    The booth-stopper, and already a proven card. A dusky purple-brown beefsteak whose dark "bruised" shoulders read as moody human skin for the morph, carrying a Cherokee-heritage story. Super-power: fresh, raw slicing — and the tagline writes itself: Ugly Outside, Legendary Inside.

  3. 3

    Glass Gem Corn Corn

    Pure kid-wow, and already a proven card. A flint/popcorn corn with translucent, rainbow-jeweled kernels, bred by Carl "White Eagle" Barnes — a part-Cherokee Oklahoma farmer — from ancestral Cherokee, Osage, and Pawnee corns. Jeweled kernels become beaded skin, silk becomes hair, husk becomes a cloak: a showstopper in every style.

  4. 4

    Music Garlic Garlic

    The most reliable hardneck for a cold-winter farm, and it doubles down on your specialty. A Porcelain type with big, bold, easy-peel cloves that thrives in 6b. Fat white cloves give a clean, heroic silhouette, and the spring scapes add another story beat.

  5. 5

    Chioggia Beet Root

    The best "interior reveal" in the set. An Italian heirloom from the coast near Venice; slice it and you get a hypnotic candy-cane bullseye of pink-and-white rings (they fade when boiled — so the card teaches "slice it raw"). Sweet, mild, and unforgettable on a card.

  6. 6

    Jimmy Nardello Pepper Pepper

    One of the best stories in the set, and already a proven card. A sweet Italian frying pepper from Basilicata; the Nardello family carried the seed to Connecticut in 1887 and named it for their son Jimmy, who gave it to Seed Savers before he died in 1983. It looks like a hot chili but is very mild and sweet at every stage — "looks hot, tastes sweet." Super-power: frying.

  7. 7

    German Extra Hardy Garlic Garlic

    The winter-proof workhorse — a Porcelain hardneck famous for exceptional cold-hardiness, a safe bet through an Ozark winter. Big cloves and strong papery wrappers make the translucent-skin morph, and the spring scapes return for another card.

  8. 8

    Black Krim Tomato Tomato

    The darkest, most dramatic tomato on the list. A beefsteak from the Crimean Peninsula with deep mahogany-to-near-black shoulders — moody, almost-bruised skin that's a gift for the uncanny morph. Rich, slightly salty flavor and a real place-name story; a perfect visual counterweight to the brighter reds.

  9. 9

    Fish Pepper Pepper

    A striped standout with real history — an African-American heirloom tied to the Chesapeake oyster houses, with variegated cream-and-green leaves and striped fruit that ripens orange to red. The mottled, patterned flesh reads as eerie, otherworldly skin, and the plant is pretty enough to be its own card.

  10. 10

    Jarrahdale Pumpkin Squash

    The fairytale head of the set — a blue-grey Australian heirloom with heavy ribs and a drum-like, flattened form, a natural skull/helmet silhouette for a dramatic morph. Sweet, stringless flesh backs the looks, and it's a star for the autumn market weeks.

  11. 11

    Lacinato ("Dinosaur") Kale Green

    The name sells itself to a 9-year-old. An 18th-century Tuscan heirloom (cavolo nero) that Thomas Jefferson grew at Monticello; its long, bumpy, blue-green "reptile hide" leaves make a dino/creature morph and drape like a cloak or wings. Cold-hardy and sweeter after frost, so it carries the cool weeks.

  12. 12

    Mortgage Lifter Tomato Tomato

    The best built-in name story here. Bred in the 1930s by West Virginia radiator repairman M.C. "Radiator Charlie" Byles, who crossed the biggest tomatoes he could find and sold plants for a dollar until the sales paid off his mortgage. Huge, meaty, mild pink-red beefsteaks — the name is the card.

  13. 13

    Rouge Vif d'Étampes ("Cinderella") Pumpkin Squash

    The literal fairytale-carriage pumpkin — a French heirloom popular in 1880s Paris, vivid red-orange, deeply ribbed and flattened, the exact shape that earned the "Cinderella" nickname. An instant Storybook or Mystic hero and a showpiece for the fall weeks.

  14. 14

    Rainbow Swiss Chard Green

    The most electric color in the greens — neon stems in yellow, pink, orange, and crimson with glowing leaf veins that read as circulatory, almost X-ray detail on a card. Fast, heat- and cold-tolerant, and cut-and-come-again, so it can reappear across many market weeks.

  15. 15

    Inchelium Red Garlic Garlic

    Rounds out your garlic range and anchors the warm (7a) end. A softneck landrace from Washington's Colville Reservation; softnecks suit milder winters and cure to the longest storage, plus they braid beautifully — a photogenic garlic-braid card and a neat teaching point about hardneck vs. softneck.

Shape of the list: 10 garlics (hardneck + softneck), plus tomatoes, peppers, roots, greens, squash, beans, corn, herbs, and a few melon/cucumber/pea wildcards — spread across the color wheel and across the season, from early peas and radishes to autumn pumpkins. It's a menu. Pick the ~12–18 that feel like your first season.
